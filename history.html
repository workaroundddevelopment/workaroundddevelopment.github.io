Workaround Driven Development

About This Manifesto

A brief and probably unnecessary history of how WDD came to exist.

It Started with Meetings

There was no conference. No ski resort. No carefully selected group of methodology experts.

There was a group of colleagues working at Workaround Industries. They worked on the same floor, but in very different parts of the organization.

Depending on the week, there might be seven people around. Or twelve. Sometimes seventeen.

Their calendars were full of meetings.

The Walk to Lunch

Every week, around noon, they would leave for lunch together.

They usually had meetings scheduled for that time. But talking to colleagues sitting a few meters away was often more valuable than another meeting on a screen.

So they went to lunch.

It was almost a rebellious act.

And, inevitably, they talked about work.

Someone would mention a TPM asking for an ETA on scope creep. Someone else would talk about signing off a feature with known bugs because the deadline mattered more. Another would mention a security exception because migrating the legacy system would take too long.

Different situations, same pattern: one priority was chosen over another because something had to move.

The Pattern

They realized that bad decisions were being made.

Not necessarily by bad people. Often by people doing exactly what the business was asking them to do: deliver faster, meet the deadline, get the feature out.

The problem was that the business would not be the one maintaining the codebase when the next requirement arrived.

A shortcut that saved two weeks today could mean a rewrite later. And when the next requirement came, the question would still be:

"What's the fastest way to get this done?"

So another workaround would be found.

Until eventually, the whole thing came down.

"It worked until it didn't."

The Next Day

The following morning, someone asked an AI to turn the lunch conversation into a manifesto.

Naturally, nobody objected.

There was a deadline.

The draft was surprisingly close.

They edited it, added a few principles and arrived at something that sounded ridiculous enough to be true.

"Deadlines over quality."
"Frameworks over design."
"Exceptions over security."

And eventually:

"Every security exception is temporary."
Especially the ones nobody remembers creating.

The Website

Then they needed somewhere to put the manifesto.

Nobody wanted to build a platform for it.

So they created a static website and put it on GitHub Pages.

Fast, cheap and almost no infrastructure.

"We'll replace it with something proper later."

They never did.

The workaround worked.

And That Is How WDD Was Born

There was no grand design.

Just a series of reasonable decisions made under understandable constraints.

Each one solved the immediate problem.

Each one pushed the real problem a little further into the future.

"Today's workaround is tomorrow's architecture."

And that is Workaround Driven Development.

Postscript

Some details of this history may have been simplified, reconstructed or improved for narrative purposes.

The meetings, however, are entirely plausible.

The workarounds are everywhere.
